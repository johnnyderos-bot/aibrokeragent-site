Compliance Issue Found HIGH SEVERITY
The AI identified a problem with how this plan defines "compensation." The plan counts only base salary ($82,000) — but Sergeant Park's actual earnings were $113,000 because he earned $31,000 in overtime. That $31,000 is being left out of his benefit calculation.
Why this matters under federal law:
The IRS has three approved ways to define "compensation" in a pension plan. Under all three, overtime generally must be included — or the plan must use the full W-2 amount. A definition that simply excludes overtime does not qualify. This means the plan's formula may be systematically understating retirement benefits for every officer and firefighter who earns overtime pay.
⚠ This is not limited to Sgt. Park. Any plan participant who earns significant overtime may have an understated benefit projection. This is a plan-level drafting issue that requires actuarial and legal review.
What the Plan Says
Plan compensation definition: "Base salary only — overtime, shift differentials, and supplemental pay are excluded."

This definition was used to calculate the Final Average Compensation (FAC) figure in the benefit formula. The result: Sgt. Park's pension is calculated on $82,000 instead of his actual $113,000 in wages.
What the IRS Requires
Plans that want to use a simplified compensation definition must adopt one of the IRS "safe harbor" definitions. All three approved alternatives include overtime or use the full W-2 amount. Excluding overtime outright does not qualify under any of them.

Plans with a non-qualifying definition face additional annual testing requirements — and benefit calculations may need to be corrected retroactively.

Question Asked
Firefighter Johnson works full-time for Millbrook FD and part-time for Riverside Township FD. Do his pension benefits from both employers need to be combined when checking the federal annual limit?
AI Answer — Citing Federal Law
The Rule (Internal Revenue Code §415(f)): When a participant earns pension benefits from more than one plan, the benefits from all plans must be added together. The total combined benefit must stay within the federal annual limit — not each plan individually.
Applied to Firefighter Johnson:
• Millbrook FD benefit: 2.5% × 16 years × $72,000 = $28,800/year
• Riverside Township FD benefit: calculated separately based on part-time service at $18,000/yr
• Both amounts must be added together when checking against the federal limit
• 2026 federal limit: $285,000/year or 100% of highest three-year average pay — whichever is less
✓ Answer: Yes — aggregation is required under federal law. At Johnson's current benefit levels he is well within the limit. This should be rechecked annually as his years of service increase at both employers.
